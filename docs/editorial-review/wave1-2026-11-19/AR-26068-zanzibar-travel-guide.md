## Short introduction

Zanzibar is a semi-autonomous part of Tanzania known for its Indian Ocean islands, Swahili coastal culture, historic Stone Town and beaches. First-time visitors often combine one or two nights in Stone Town with several nights on the coast. Choose the beach area by tide, atmosphere and transport needs, and verify current entry, insurance and travel advice before booking.

Zanzibar's appeal is its contrast. A visitor might spend one morning walking through a compact historic town and the next watching fishing boats on a broad tidal beach. That variety makes it easy to misjudge accommodation: a hotel can be advertised as “Zanzibar beachfront” yet sit a long drive from the activities, restaurants or swimming conditions you actually want.

This guide separates the islands' main decisions—where to base yourself, how long to stay, what to see and what practical details to verify—from the glossy promise that every beach is equally swimmable at every hour.

## 1. Where exactly is Zanzibar?

Zanzibar is not an independent country. It is a semi-autonomous part of the United Republic of Tanzania. The name is commonly used for the Zanzibar Archipelago and often specifically for Unguja, the principal island where Zanzibar City and Stone Town are located. Pemba is another important island in the archipelago, with a different pace and travel infrastructure.

The islands lie off the East African mainland in the Indian Ocean. Visitors commonly arrive by air or from Dar es Salaam by ferry, but schedules, weather and passenger arrangements must be checked in advance. A hotel on one side of Unguja may be far from an airport or ferry terminal in driving terms.

For an itinerary combining Zanzibar with a mainland safari, think of the islands as a *separate travel segment*. Treat the transfer day realistically, particularly if small-aircraft connections, luggage restrictions or a ferry are involved. Do not promise that a morning game drive and a distant beach check-in will fit a particular date without verified schedules.

## 2. Stone Town: history beyond the postcard

**Stone Town of Zanzibar** was inscribed on UNESCO's World Heritage List in 2000. UNESCO describes an urban fabric shaped by cultural exchange among Africa, the Arab region, India and Europe. The town is not merely a collection of picturesque doors; it is a living urban environment with homes, markets, places of worship and significant historical sites.

A thoughtful visit includes walking through the old streets with a qualified local guide who can explain the architecture, trading connections, lived history and sensitive legacy of the slave trade. Give the area time rather than treating it as an hour-long photo stop on the way to the beach.

Respect residents and religious spaces. Ask permission before photographing people, avoid obstructing entrances and dress with local custom in mind. Historic buildings may undergo conservation work, and some sites can be restricted or temporarily closed. UNESCO has documented the importance of protecting Stone Town's heritage and managing tourism pressures.

The town is particularly useful for travellers interested in Swahili history, architecture, markets and cuisine. It is a less appropriate base for someone whose principal aim is to spend every day on a quiet, easily accessible beach. In that case, stay on the coast and plan a deliberate excursion to the historic centre.

## 3. North coast: Nungwi and Kendwa

Nungwi and Kendwa are among the best-known northern beach areas. They appeal to visitors who want a more developed tourism scene, beach activities, restaurants and a choice of resorts. The exact atmosphere varies by stretch of coast and property; do not assume all northern accommodation is lively or all is quiet.

North-coast geography is worth investigating if you hope to swim at many times of day. Zanzibar is strongly tidal, but the experience varies locally. Some northern stretches are commonly favoured for more convenient swimming conditions than broad east-coast flats; tides, waves, seasonal seaweed and local beach conditions still require daily attention.

For a family, investigate shallow-water conditions, lifeguard provision and access between the room and shore. For a couple seeking privacy, check whether the property shares a busy beach with many boat operators or nightlife venues. The same area can offer radically different experiences a short distance apart.

Allow transfer time from Stone Town or the airport. An apparently inexpensive property can become less appealing if repeated long taxi journeys consume the holiday.

## 4. East and southeast: Paje, Jambiani and quieter stays

Paje and Jambiani are associated with wide beaches, tidal flats and a different rhythm from the northern resorts. Paje attracts interest from watersport travellers, including kitesurfing when conditions allow. Jambiani can suit visitors who value village context, a quieter atmosphere and long beach walks.

The key decision is whether you want **tidal scenery** or **easy swimming throughout the day**. At low tide, parts of the eastern coast can expose broad shallows. This is beautiful for exploring the landscape with appropriate local guidance, but can frustrate travellers expecting deep water directly outside the room at all times.

Conditions change with tide tables, monsoon winds and season. Before paying for an east-coast hotel, ask the property for honest photographs at low tide, a description of safe swimming access, and the travel time to restaurants and other activities. Check whether the beach is rocky, sandy or affected by seaweed at your dates.

A lower room rate does not always compensate for an unsuitable location. The best coast for a kitesurfer is not automatically the best coast for a family with toddlers or a traveller who rarely leaves the hotel.

## 5. Things to do beyond lying on the beach

Stone Town is the strongest cultural anchor for a first visit. Other commonly marketed outings include spice-oriented experiences, sailing, snorkelling, diving and nearby island excursions. The quality and environmental impact of these activities depend on the operator, weather and conservation rules.

Ask snorkelling or boat operators about life jackets, qualifications, vessel capacity, safety briefings and protected-area procedures. Avoid experiences that encourage touching reefs, chasing wildlife, crowding animals or feeding marine species for a photograph. Choose a trip because it is responsibly run, not because the brochure guarantees a sighting.

Spice-related visits can provide context for the island's trading history and local agriculture when led by knowledgeable hosts. Verify what is genuinely included. A short demonstration, agricultural visit and full culinary class are not interchangeable products.

If time permits, combine one guided historic-town day, one or two carefully selected coastal activities and unstructured beach time. People often enjoy Zanzibar most when the itinerary leaves room for the location rather than moving between attractions every hour.

## 6. When to visit and how long to stay

Broadly, visitors often favour relatively drier stretches of the year for beach activities and travel, while wetter periods can bring heavier rain and disruptions. Zanzibar's coastal weather and the Serengeti's savanna conditions should not be treated as identical merely because both are in Tanzania.

The traditional wetter periods often discussed in regional travel guidance fall around March to May and parts of November to December, with local variation. A particular month cannot guarantee dry skies or calm seas. Dive and kitesurfing conditions also depend on winds and sea state, which are not identical from one coast to another.

For first-time visitors, four to six nights can provide a useful combination of Stone Town and one beach base. Add more time if you want diving, cultural outings or a relaxed beach stay. With a short trip, choosing one coastal base and making a planned day trip is often better than changing hotels repeatedly.

A practical six-night outline is one or two nights near Stone Town, followed by four or five nights in a beach area chosen for your actual preferences. Keep a travel buffer before long-haul flights. Domestic transfers and ferry connections deserve contingency planning.

## 7. Entry, insurance, health and safety

Check current immigration and travel-insurance rules for the exact passport and journey. The UK FCDO currently provides entry requirements for Tanzania and Zanzibar and advises travellers to verify relevant insurance obligations. Regulations can change, and commercial travel packages may omit a mandatory government-provided product even when they include private insurance.

Government travel advisories should be read on the day of booking and shortly before departure. The US State Department issued Tanzania-specific travel advice in October 2026, and the UK FCDO maintains regional warnings and safety guidance. A country-level warning does not mean every location has an identical risk profile, but travellers should not dismiss official guidance.

The CDC's Tanzania traveller-health information includes malaria prevention considerations. Speak with a qualified travel-health clinician, particularly for children, pregnancy, chronic conditions or trips mixing coastal and mainland destinations. Bring adequate prescribed medicines and know how to access medical help.

Use established transport providers, keep a record of your accommodation and onward travel, and check ferry operating conditions. The US State Department advises care when selecting ferry tickets and travel arrangements. Do not assume an informal operator has the same safety procedures as a licensed provider.

## FAQs

**Is Zanzibar a country?** No. Zanzibar is a semi-autonomous part of the United Republic of Tanzania.

**Where is Zanzibar?** It is in the Indian Ocean off Tanzania's East African coast; Unguja is the island many visitors mean when they say Zanzibar.

**Is Stone Town worth staying in?** Yes for culture, history and a walkable urban atmosphere. For a beach-centred trip, combine it with a coastal base.

**Where should I stay for swimming?** Compare the precise shoreline, tides and property access. Northern areas such as Nungwi and Kendwa may better suit some swimmers than very tidal east-coast stretches.

**Is Zanzibar safe?** Risks vary by location, activity and current conditions. Consult official travel advice and practise ordinary travel and water-safety precautions.

**Is Zanzibar expensive?** Costs range widely by accommodation, season and activities. Compare full-day transport, meal and transfer costs instead of relying on a single “cheap island” label.
