## Short introduction

Biltong is a South African-style dried meat, often prepared by salting and seasoning strips of beef or game and drying them under controlled conditions. It differs from many types of American jerky in its cutting, acid-and-spice treatment and lower-temperature drying approach. Both are preserved meats, but neither is automatically safe or healthier simply because it is dried.

For people encountering the name for the first time, biltong can look deceptively familiar: strips of dark meat in a packet or a butcher's display. Its character becomes clearer with the first bite. Depending on how it has been prepared and sliced, it may be tender or firm, with a savoury, sometimes tangy flavour and a spice profile frequently associated with coriander and pepper.

Biltong is a food tradition, not a trademark or a single uniform manufacturing standard. Recipes and commercial products differ. The defining distinction from jerky is better understood through preparation and texture than through sweeping claims that one is categorically superior.

## 1. What is biltong made from?

Beef is widely associated with biltong, but the product can also be made from other meats, including some game species where sourcing and regulations permit. Meat cuts, fat content, slice thickness and processing all affect the final product.

A familiar preparation approach involves salt, vinegar and seasonings, often including coriander and black pepper. These ingredients help form the characteristic flavour, while salt, acidification and drying can act as barriers to microbial growth when correctly managed. A South African scientific review describes the role of salting, spicing, vinegar and drying in biltong production.

There is no single seasoning formula accepted across every household or producer. Some products emphasise coriander; others are pepper-forward, spicy, sweeter or milder. Some contain preservatives or ingredients that are not obvious from appearance. If an ingredient matters for allergy, dietary, religious or medical reasons, check the label rather than assuming a “traditional” recipe.

The choice of cut also changes the eating experience. Thin, lean slices are not identical to thicker pieces retaining some fat. Because biltong is usually sold at different dryness levels, describing it only as “hard dried beef” misses the variety consumers encounter.

## 2. How biltong is traditionally prepared

At a general level, biltong production involves selecting and cutting suitable meat, applying a salt-and-seasoning treatment, introducing vinegar or other acidification in many recipes, and drying under controlled conditions. The precise sequence varies.

Academic literature on South African biltong identifies multiple factors that determine product quality: raw meat hygiene, salt concentration, acid levels, air movement, drying conditions and final water activity. Commercial producers rely on validated methods and controls rather than taste alone.

A particularly important distinction is that biltong is commonly dried at lower temperatures than many jerky processes. The UK's Food Standards Agency notes that jerky is often dried at higher temperatures or smoked or cooked before drying. These patterns are descriptive, not absolute rules: both products exist in many styles.

Home food preparation is not risk-free. An attractive dried surface does not prove the centre of a product is free of pathogens. Research on biltong microbiology discusses hazards associated with inadequate processing and handling. For this reason, we do not give an untested home-preservation time, temperature or vinegar ratio here. Anyone making dried meat should follow a food-safety-validated process that applies to the meat and equipment used.

## 3. Biltong versus jerky: a useful comparison

**Cut and shape.** Biltong is often prepared as larger or thicker strips that may be cut into smaller slices after drying. Jerky is often prepared from thinner slices, although exceptions exist.

**Seasoning.** Biltong commonly features salt, vinegar, coriander and pepper. Jerky is frequently marinated, sometimes with sweet or smoky flavours, but its recipes are also very diverse.

**Drying approach.** Many biltong styles use lower-temperature drying as part of a hurdle-based preservation process; jerky often includes more heat, smoking or a cooking step. This is why copying a jerky method without adjustment—or copying a biltong recipe in an uncontrolled environment—can be unsafe.

**Texture.** Biltong can be comparatively soft, chewy, fatty or dry depending on the cut and finishing preference. Jerky is often chewier and more uniform in thin strips, but label and product variation remain important.

**Where it is associated.** Biltong has a strong South African identity; jerky is strongly associated with North American dried-meat traditions. Neither belongs exclusively to one country, and both are widely sold elsewhere.

A shopper comparing the two should judge an actual product by ingredient list, producer, safety standards and personal taste—not by marketing promises about ancient or “chemical-free” methods.

## 4. How does biltong taste, and how do people eat it?

People often describe biltong as savoury, peppery and lightly tangy. Coriander gives some versions a distinctive aromatic character. The level of salt can be noticeable, particularly in highly seasoned products.

Biltong is commonly eaten as a snack, sometimes accompanied by other savoury foods. It can also be sliced into sandwiches or incorporated into simple dishes, provided the producer's storage instructions are followed. There is no universal “correct” thickness: a butcher may offer chunky, moist, dry or thinly sliced versions.

For a first tasting, choose a reputable producer and a modest amount. Compare a plain or classic style with a spicier version only if desired. If you dislike very dry meat, ask about the product's texture before buying.

Avoid interpreting visible colour alone as a measure of freshness or safety. Different meats, drying levels, seasonings, packaging and lighting can produce different appearances. A safe-looking slice can still be mishandled; an unfamiliar appearance is not in itself a diagnosis.

## 5. Is biltong healthy?

Biltong is a meat product and can provide protein. That fact does not make every packet a suitable choice for every person's diet. Sodium levels, fat content, preservatives, portion sizes and the type of meat can differ substantially.

South African research has examined ways to reduce sodium while retaining safety. That is a useful reminder that preservation and nutrition cannot be considered independently: changing salt or processing conditions may require additional safety controls.

It is misleading to call all biltong “healthy” or “safer than jerky” without comparing measured products and dietary context. For consumers managing blood pressure, allergies or other medical needs, the relevant nutritional label and professional advice matter more than a generic social-media claim.

Likewise, “natural,” “handmade” or “traditional” is not a proof of pathogen control. Commercial products should be manufactured and stored under appropriate food hygiene standards. People who are pregnant, immunocompromised or otherwise vulnerable to foodborne illness should take particular care with ready-to-eat meat and consult current food-safety guidance.

## 6. What to check when buying or storing biltong

Read the ingredient list and any allergen declaration. Check the manufacturer's identity, expiry or best-before information, storage conditions and whether the packaging is intact. For online purchases, verify the business's handling practices and delivery conditions, especially in hot climates.

Packaged biltong may have specific storage instructions. Do not assume a product can be stored indefinitely at room temperature simply because it looks dry. Moisture, packaging damage and improper handling affect stability.

A retailer should be able to explain whether the product is ready to eat, how it should be stored after opening and what its shelf-life instructions mean. For homemade or small-batch products, ask how food safety is controlled rather than relying on a seller's confidence.

If the product has been improperly stored, damaged, mouldy or otherwise suspect, do not try to make it safe by adding seasoning or briefly warming it. Follow reputable food-safety advice and discard unsafe food.

## 7. Why biltong deserves a place in African food coverage

Biltong is part of a much broader landscape of African food preservation and regional cuisines. It offers a useful case study in how practical food preparation becomes a distinct culinary identity. Yet an editorial article should not romanticise the process at the expense of safety or claim that one country's technique explains every dried-meat tradition across the continent.

For readers learning about South African food, biltong provides an accessible entry point into snack culture and meat preservation. For people comparing products in shops abroad, a factual distinction from jerky is more useful than an unsupported nutrition ranking. This page therefore focuses on definition, process, taste and safe purchasing, rather than publishing an unvalidated recipe.

## FAQs

**Is biltong raw meat?** It is dried meat and often does not undergo the same cooking process as many jerkies. That does not mean it is unprocessed. Safe commercial production depends on validated controls.

**Is biltong always beef?** Beef is common, but other meats may be used. Check the product label.

**Why does biltong often taste tangy?** Vinegar or acidification is common in many preparations, along with salt and spices.

**Can I make biltong at home?** It is possible, but safe dried-meat production needs validated controls; do not follow unverified online drying instructions.

**Is biltong gluten-free?** Some recipes may be, but gluten status depends on ingredients, flavourings and cross-contact. Read the label and ask the manufacturer.
