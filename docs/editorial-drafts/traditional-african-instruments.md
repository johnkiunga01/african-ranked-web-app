# Traditional African Instruments: A Guide to Their Sounds, Making and Meanings

**Matched CMS draft:** #51, *Traditional African Instruments: A Guide*. **Editorial status:** Research manuscript only; not saved to CMS, reviewed or published. **Research date:** 7 November 2026. **Search focus:** traditional African musical instruments. **Suggested SEO title:** Traditional African Musical Instruments: Mbira, Kora, Balafon and Drums. **Suggested meta description:** Explore how the mbira, kora, balafon, talking drum and other African instruments are made and played, with museum and UNESCO sources. **Important:** Source collection records do not grant reuse rights for their photographs or recordings.

Traditional African instruments are as varied as the communities and musical traditions of the continent. The mbira or sansi uses plucked metal tongues, the kora belongs to West African stringed traditions, and the balafon is a tuned percussion instrument. Yoruba dundun drums can change pitch as their strings are pressed. These are not interchangeable symbols of a single “African sound”: each has a particular construction, repertoire, setting and history that deserves to be understood on its own terms.

## How to understand traditional African instruments

A useful way to begin is with **how an instrument makes sound**. Strings vibrate on a kora; metal tongues vibrate on a mbira-family instrument; wooden bars are struck on a balafon; a stretched membrane vibrates on a drum. These mechanisms help listeners identify what they hear without reducing the instrument to an exotic-looking museum object. The same broad sound-making family may contain many local names, tunings and playing techniques, so a Western orchestral classification cannot replace community-specific vocabulary.

The next question is **where and by whom it is played**. A musical instrument might accompany storytelling, a ritual, a celebration, personal recreation or a public performance. The function can shift as musicians carry traditions into different settings. A museum catalogue can tell readers what an object is made from and how it was collected, but an instrument's ongoing meaning is also carried by living performers and makers. Avoid generalising that every drum is a ceremonial object or that all plucked instruments are used for meditation.

Finally, distinguish historical descriptions from present practice. UNESCO's intangible heritage records focus on knowledge transmitted by people, not just collections of old objects. In its account of [Mbira/Sansi making and playing in Malawi and Zimbabwe](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/art-of-crafting-and-playing-mbira-sansi-the-finger-plucking-traditional-musical-instrument-in-malawi-and-zimbabwe-01541?RL=01541), UNESCO describes learning in families as well as schools and organised instruction. A tradition is not frozen in the past just because a museum owns an early example.

A responsible overview also avoids presenting a single country as the exclusive origin of an instrument used across borders. Musical histories include movement, adaptation and related but distinct versions. If a source identifies an instrument as “possibly” from a community, repeat that uncertainty. If a source is a catalogue of books rather than the books' full contents, use it for bibliography, not as proof of every claim made by the author.

## Mbira and sansi: music made by vibrating keys

The mbira and related instruments are often described as **lamellophones**: sound is produced by plucking or depressing tongues, often made of metal, attached to a wooden board or resonating box. The [Metropolitan Museum's nyonganyonga object record](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/503679) explains how vibrating tongues are tuned by changes in their effective length, and how a hollow resonator or attached jingling elements can change the sound. The museum also notes that instruments in this family have multiple local names.

UNESCO's recognition of Mbira/Sansi crafting and playing in Malawi and Zimbabwe offers a closer view of living practice. Its [intangible heritage record](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/art-of-crafting-and-playing-mbira-sansi-the-finger-plucking-traditional-musical-instrument-in-malawi-and-zimbabwe-01541?RL=01541) describes metal keys attached to a board, sometimes combined with a resonator, and an approach in which repeated phrases vary and overlap. That description matters because a mbira performance cannot be understood only by counting the keys: the patterns created between notes and the musicians matter as well.

It is tempting to call every instrument of this family a “thumb piano,” but that phrase can obscure local classifications. A lamellophone is not built like a piano, and its performance tradition should not be treated as a miniature version of European keyboard music. The Met's collection includes a [Chokwe kisaanji from Angola](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/501102), another example of how names and forms vary across regions. An article can explain shared mechanisms while still preserving those distinctions.

UNESCO also describes social messages and the transmission of musical knowledge through Mbira/Sansi playing. Do not claim that every performance has one sacred or therapeutic effect; those are different kinds of statements that require evidence. Some contexts may be ceremonial, others educational or recreational. Readers will understand the instrument better by noticing how tone, resonance and interlocking phrases work than by attaching an unverified universal meaning to it.

If listening for the first time, concentrate on the relationship between a recurring pattern and the small differences from one cycle to the next. A second line of notes may sound as if another instrument has entered even when one player is creating interlocking parts. Compare recordings with adequate contextual information, and be alert to differences in tuning between related instruments rather than expecting one standard scale.

## Kora: strings, Mande music and interwoven lines

The kora is associated with West African musical traditions, including Mande performance communities. It is commonly described as a harp-lute, with strings and a large resonating body, though details vary by instrument and maker. Unlike a guitar, its arrangement of strings encourages distinctive patterns and the interplay of bass, accompaniment and melodic lines. The Smithsonian holds archival recordings by Gambian kora performer [Alhaji Bai Konte](https://www.si.edu/object/archives/components/sova-cfch-rinz-ref7467), a useful documented example of the instrument and its recorded performance tradition.

Because the kora is often described in relation to griots or jali traditions, readers should understand that the musicians are not simply entertainers hired to play background melodies. In different communities, performers may have roles in memory, praise, narration and the communication of family or community histories. Exactly how those roles work depends on time and place; it is not appropriate to assign one social role to all West African musicians. The Smithsonian's catalogue entry for Lucy Durán's [*The Kora: From West Africa to the World Stage*](https://www.si.edu/object/siris_sil_1093651) points to dedicated scholarship on this history, though a catalogue entry alone is not the entire article.

The kora also appears in contemporary performances and international collaborations. Its presence in modern musical settings does not make it less traditional, and an acoustic instrument is not automatically more culturally authentic than an amplified one. Focus on the musician's approach, the repertoire and the credited relationships between artists. A short online video may showcase a spectacular technique without explaining the context of a piece; a fuller recording or interview is better for introducing the form.

For a beginner, listening carefully to several parts of one performance can be more informative than memorising instrument dimensions. Notice a repeated accompaniment pattern, the movement of an upper melodic line and the relationship with any vocal material. A performer may shape a gradual musical argument even where there is no conventional pop-song verse and chorus. Avoid the assumption that complex melodic music must be written down to be sophisticated.

When presenting kora examples in an article, credit the specific performer and recording if that information is available. Do not embed or redistribute a Smithsonian archival recording based solely on its catalogue listing. The Smithsonian specifically notes restrictions on research playback and reproduction for some archived material. Reference links and media-reuse permission are separate issues.

## Balafon: tuned wooden bars and cultural memory

The balafon is commonly described as an African xylophone: tuned wooden bars are struck, and instruments may use calabash resonators beneath the bars. But this broad description should not erase distinct regional designs. UNESCO's account of the [Sosso-Bala cultural space in Guinea](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/cultural-space-of-sosso-bala-00009?RL=00009) documents a specific revered instrument, associated knowledge and a community tradition of custodianship. It is not a template that explains every balafon across West Africa.

UNESCO describes the Sosso-Bala as an instrument of particular significance in the Mandingue cultural setting. Its record ties the instrument to oral history, transmission of musical knowledge and particular custodians. Those details are culturally significant, but they should not be converted into unsupported claims about what every balafon player may do or who is permitted to play every version. An individual sacred instrument and a broader instrument family must be kept distinct.

The sound-making principle is straightforward to explain without flattening the culture. A performer strikes bars that are tuned to different pitches. Resonators can alter how the notes project and sound. Patterns of melody and rhythm may be interwoven, including with other musicians or voices. The musical interest lies in relationships between notes and players, not simply in the number of bars. The specific tuning and repertoire belong to the tradition being discussed.

The [UNESCO safeguarding project for Sosso-Bala](https://ich.unesco.org/en/projects/safeguarding-of-the-intangible-cultural-heritage-of-the-sosso-bala-00009?projectID=00009&project_id=00009) also addresses teaching, recording and the transmission of expertise. That is a reminder that preserving music cannot be accomplished by displaying a beautiful instrument alone. Makers, custodians and learners are part of the tradition. A good article should tell readers where the source makes a specific claim and avoid treating a museum-style photograph as sufficient evidence of living practice.

For comparison listening, notice how the attack of a struck wooden key differs from the sustaining and vibrating sound of mbira tongues or kora strings. Yet do not assign rigid emotional labels such as “joyful,” “mystical” or “tribal” to a sound without context. A recording may be joyful, solemn, experimental or practical depending on the piece, its setting and the listener.

## Talking drums: pitch, language and rhythmic communication

“Talking drum” is a descriptive English label that covers more than one instrument and tradition. An important example is the Yoruba **dundun** family. The Smithsonian's National Museum of African Art describes an [iya ilu dundun](https://music.si.edu/smithsonian-institution-unit/national-museum-african-art), an hourglass-shaped drum whose pitch changes when the performer squeezes the tension cords while striking the drum. This makes it possible to approximate tones and cadences associated with Yoruba speech. The mechanism and the connection to a tonal language are more helpful facts than an unsupported claim that the drum can literally say anything.

The pitch can move rather than remain fixed because the tension on the drumhead changes as the cords are pressed. A performer combines these pitch changes with patterns of timing and phrasing. Understanding the system requires familiarity with language, musical conventions and performance context. A listener unfamiliar with Yoruba may hear an impressive rhythmic passage while missing the linguistic relationships that an experienced listener recognises.

Avoid saying that every African drum is a talking drum. Drums differ in body shape, membranes, playing method, tuning and community use. Some are primarily used for dance, ensemble accompaniment, signalling or other roles, sometimes with overlapping purposes. Even within the Yoruba dundun family, instrument types and musical roles differ. The Smithsonian source concerns a particular documented object, not a universal percussion standard for the continent.

A useful comparison is between a fixed-pitch percussion instrument and one in which pitch can be manipulated in performance. That difference helps readers understand why the dundun is distinctive. Yet a description of how the strings work should not be presented as a comprehensive explanation of Yoruba musical knowledge. When discussing the language and repertoire, cite specialists and living musicians rather than guessing at verbal translations from audio.

In an educational article, illustrations of the tension cords, drumheads and curved beater could help, provided their provenance and licences are verified. A product photo with no cultural context may illustrate the general shape but not the particular tradition. Do not claim an anonymous photograph depicts a dundun from a named community without identifying evidence.

## Other instrument families worth exploring

The four familiar examples above are only a small portion of African instrumental diversity. The Smithsonian's National Museum of African Art collection also describes a [Fang ngombi harp](https://music.si.edu/smithsonian-institution-unit/national-museum-african-art) associated in its object record with a specific religious context. This should be understood as a particular harp tradition, not proof that all African harps share the same meanings or construction. Related string instruments can differ substantially even when their names are translated into the same English category.

The Metropolitan Museum preserves examples of additional plucked-key instruments, including a [Chokwe kisaanji](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/501102) and a [possibly Cameroonian zanze](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/502185). The word “possibly” is important: the catalogue marks uncertainty about the object's attribution. A careful website should repeat that qualification rather than state the provenance as settled. Material, approximate period and collection history provide useful context but are not a substitute for the maker's testimony.

Musical instruments can also be understood through how they form ensembles. A drum may have a role alongside singing, dance or other drums. A string instrument may accompany storytelling or stand as a focus of close listening. A lamellophone may be played alone or with other musicians. Rather than inventing a pan-African ensemble, explain each combination through a named recording or documented performance tradition.

This also explains why a list of “top African instruments” is often editorially weaker than a guide to different types. Any numbered popularity ranking would require a defined dataset and comparable evidence about how instruments are played or distributed. Without such evidence, descriptive categories are more honest and more useful. The aim here is to introduce the listener to mechanisms, regional histories and people, not to declare one instrument the continent's most important.

## Listening, learning and crediting performers responsibly

Begin with one documented performance from each instrument family. Before pressing play, read the credit line, date and location attached to the recording. Those details help explain what you are hearing without encouraging stereotypes. When a museum archive includes uncertain information, keep the uncertainty visible. Do not infer an individual performer's community, motivations or personal history from appearance or an unlabelled photograph.

When learning an instrument, a local teacher, performer or specialist curriculum can offer knowledge that a generic internet diagram cannot. Tuning, phrasing, repertoire and the meanings of music may be passed through direct instruction. UNESCO's documentation of Mbira/Sansi and Sosso-Bala shows that the transmission of expertise is a central part of safeguarding these practices. An article can point to that principle without promising that every tradition is taught in exactly the same way.

The rights to recordings, photographs and sheet music also matter. The Smithsonian archive entry for Alhaji Bai Konte's recording notes restrictions on duplication. It is fine to identify the record and link to its catalogue, but not to assume unrestricted permission to reproduce the sound. The same rule applies to museum object photographs and artist images. A responsible editorial workflow should keep a separate rights record for every media asset.

Finally, leave room for contemporary creativity. Makers and performers continue to modify instruments, collaborate across genres and teach through new channels. Describing a tradition as “ancient” without dates, context or recognition of current performers can erase the people sustaining it now. A strong introductory article respects inherited knowledge and the choices of today's musicians together.

## A practical guide for readers and editors

When you encounter an unfamiliar instrument, ask four questions: What vibrates to make the sound? What is the local name, and where is the particular documented example from? Who is credited as maker or performer? In what setting is it played? These questions form a reliable starting point for further research. They also prevent a common editorial mistake: using a broad English name to imply that different musical systems are all identical.

Compare the sound-making categories without ranking cultures. A mbira-family instrument relies on plucked tongues; a balafon uses struck bars; a kora uses strings; and a dundun uses a tensioned drum membrane. These physical distinctions can be demonstrated without borrowing copyrighted recordings or inventing historical details. The meaning of the music, however, must come from its particular social and historical context.

For children and first-time learners, simple demonstrations of vibration can be an accessible entry point, but physical imitation is not the same thing as learning a cultural repertoire. If a classroom activity draws on a named tradition, acknowledge the community and avoid presenting a homemade model as an authentic historical instrument. Similarly, a craft kit can teach acoustics without claiming to recreate a sacred object.

For a traveller interested in performance, consult local programmes and the musicians involved before making assumptions about access or etiquette. Not every ceremonial performance is a tourist show, and not every public concert follows ceremonial rules. Good cultural reporting respects permission, credits and the people responsible for maintaining traditions.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is an mbira the same as a kalimba?**  
These names can refer to related plucked-tongue instrument families, but they should not be treated as interchangeable in every local context. Identify the specific instrument, maker, tuning and tradition being discussed. UNESCO's Mbira/Sansi record documents particular Malawi and Zimbabwe practices.

**What is the difference between a kora and a balafon?**  
A kora is a stringed instrument associated with West African musical traditions. A balafon produces sound when tuned bars are struck. Both may support complex melodic and rhythmic patterns, but their construction and performance practices are distinct.

**How does a talking drum change pitch?**  
Some hourglass drums in the Yoruba dundun family have tension cords connecting the heads. A player squeezes the cords while striking the membrane, changing its tension and therefore the pitch. This feature can help imitate aspects of tonal speech.

**Can I use museum recordings in an article?**  
A catalogue link does not automatically grant reproduction rights. Check each recording's terms and obtain the necessary permissions before uploading, embedding or redistributing media.
